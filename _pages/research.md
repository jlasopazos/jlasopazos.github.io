## Working Papers

**[Marketization of Home Production and Female Labor Supply](/files/Jesus_Laso_Pazos_Marketization.pdf)**

<details class="abstract">
<summary>Abstract</summary>
<div class="abstract-box">
<p>Many services that households produce at home, including childcare, cooking, and cleaning, have close market substitutes, and both the home-produced and the market versions are provided disproportionately by women. This paper studies how policies that promote the marketization of home production affect gender gaps in time use and wages, and how these effects evolve over the life-cycle. I extend the static, unitary framework of Duval-Hernández, Fang, and Ngai (2023) along two dimensions that the literature has shown to matter for female labor supply: human capital accumulation and intrahousehold bargaining. I develop a multi-sector, overlapping generations, general equilibrium model with home production and a collective household under limited commitment, in which spouses accumulate human capital through market work and bargaining weights adjust endogenously. A policy that lowers the relative price of market services then operates through two channels. First, it raises current and future incentives to work, since today's market hours increase future wages and the returns to future market work. Second, it shifts the bargaining position of the spouse specialized in home production by improving that spouse's outside option. I first use a two-period version of the model to isolate these mechanisms, and then a life-cycle economy in which home-production needs, skill depreciation, and household specialization vary with age, to quantify the policy's effects and decompose the two channels over the life-cycle.</p>
<p><em>Presented at:</em> Goethe University FQMG Brown Bag (2026).</p>
</div>

**Social and Inner Disconnect and Societal Outcomes: Evidence from Panel Data** (with [Sandra Eickmeier](https://www.bundesbank.de/en/sandra-eickmeier))

<details class="abstract">
<summary>Abstract</summary>
<div class="abstract-box">
<p>This paper examines how social disconnection and consciousness—defined as a deeper awareness of oneself and reality—relate to economic behavior, political attitudes, health outcomes, and cultural engagement. Using panel data from households in the Netherlands, we show that social isolation is associated with lower consumption, poorer health, reduced institutional and interpersonal trust, a higher likelihood of supporting right-wing extremist parties, and reduced cultural engagement. We argue that social disconnection is only one facet of a deeper underlying issue: a disconnect from individuals' existential inner dimensions or core self. Building on this perspective, we introduce a novel classification of consciousness based on traditional, modern-rational, modern-hedonic, and integral value orientations. Extending the empirical analysis, we find that individuals with a modern–hedonic orientation tend to exhibit higher levels of consumption, lower institutional trust, a greater propensity to support far-right political parties, and less consistent well-being than traditional individuals, whereas individuals at an integral consciousness level display more favorable outcomes across domains. Taken together, these results suggest that both social disconnection and consciousness operate as systemic phenomena; addressing contemporary societal challenges may therefore require targeting these underlying dimensions.</p>
<p><em>Presented at:</em> Goethe University FQMG Brown Bag (2026); Bundesbank Research Centre Brown Bag (2026)*.</p>

</div>
